How the map is made

The Map tab groups the papers in the current list into topics and lays them out so that papers close in meaning sit close together. Code: litmap.py (and api_map in app.py).

  1. Papers. The current list’s papers that are in the local PubMed copy (title, and the abstract when it has one), leaving out the facet boxes on the left: on the map those grey out the papers they leave out, rather than making a new map. Above 20,000, a fixed sample of 20,000: chosen by a hash of the PMID, so it is the same sample every time and overlaps across filters.
  2. Embeddings. Each paper’s 384-number MedEmbed-small vector of its title + abstract (unit length, so cosine similarity is the dot product).
  3. Neighbours. Each paper’s 15-point neighbourhood (itself and its nearest papers, cosine), found once and shared by both UMAP runs below.
  4. UMAP to 12 dimensions (min_dist 0), for clustering.
  5. Topics: HDBSCAN on the 12-D points. Run 16 ways: both cluster selection methods (“eom” and “leaf”) × 8 minimum topic sizes (the number of papers ÷ 5, 8, 12, 20, 30, 45, 60, 80; at least 8), min_samples 5. Results with more than 8 topics are skipped. In each, papers HDBSCAN leaves out join the topic of their nearest topic paper (12-D), except those among the 10% of all papers it rates least typical (GLOSH outlier score), which stay grey (“no clear topic”). The run kept is the one with the highest score: the effective number of topics (exp of the entropy of the topic sizes) × the share of papers in a topic. Topics are numbered biggest first.
  6. UMAP to 2 dimensions (min_dist 0.1) from the same neighbours, for drawing. Topics come from the 12-D run, so a topic can look split or overlap another in 2-D.
  7. Topic words. Class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF, as in BERTopic) over the single words and word pairs of the titles and abstracts. Left out: English stop words, words every abstract uses (study, patients, results…), and the gene’s symbols (for a disease, the words of its name and of its commonest PubTator3 wordings). A pair never spans punctuation or a stop word. A term must be in at least max(3, papers ÷ 2,000) papers. Weight = the term’s share of the topic’s terms × log(1 + mean terms per class ÷ the term’s count in all classes); grey papers count as a class too. Six per topic, skipping forms of words already chosen.
  8. Topic names. An AI model gets each topic’s words and its 5 most typical titles (nearest the topic’s 12-D centre) and gives each topic a 2–5 word name. Without it, the topic’s 3 most distinctive words (words no other topic has come first).
  9. Drill in. The same steps on one topic’s papers only; the AI is told they all come from that topic.
  10. Drawing. One colour per topic; grey = no clear topic. Curated papers are drawn bigger, with a ring. Maps are saved, so a list opens instantly the second time.

Why these settings: see Methods tested.

Map Explorer

The Map Explorer tab shows the same papers and topics as the map, but places them on two axes that mean something, instead of UMAP’s layout. Each axis is a direction in the 384-number embedding space, and a paper’s place on it is the dot product of its vector with that direction (with unit vectors, the cosine). Code: api_axes in app.py, and axes.py.

  1. Your axes. Each axis is some words (embedded as a search query) or a topic (the mean of its papers’ vectors). Across is how close each paper is to the first. Up is how close it is to the second, beyond the first: the second direction with its part along the first taken out, so the two axes are at right angles. Without that, two related axes would put every paper on the diagonal. The note says how far apart the two are in meaning (90° = unrelated).
  2. Or let the map find two. Three buttons find directions from the papers themselves:
    • Discover: the two directions in which these papers differ most, compared with how much papers about human genes in general differ along them. Technically, the top generalised eigenvectors of the map’s covariance against that of 50,000 random human-gene papers (shrunk 20% towards equal spread in every direction). Before that, the map’s own direction is taken out, or it would come first for most maps. That direction runs from the average gene paper to the map’s centre.
    • Main spread: the two directions in which these papers differ most (principal components), whatever gene papers do in general.
    • About it ↔ in passing: across is the map’s own direction. Papers on the right are like the rest of the map, and papers on the left are like gene papers in general: these often mention the gene or disease only in passing, or are linked to it by mistake. Up is Discover’s first direction.
    Each axis is turned so that its newer papers are at the top or right (except the across axis of About it ↔ in passing). The three were chosen from 18 kinds of axis tried on 5 maps, where three blind judges ranked Discover first.
  3. Naming the ends. Each end of a suggested axis is its 10% of the papers. The AI model gets, for each end:
    • its most distinctive words (c-TF-IDF, as for topics), from the papers that are not also at an end of the other axis, so that each end is named by what is its own;
    • up to 8 titles from 2–10% in from it (the most extreme papers are often odd ones out), again leaving out papers shared with the other axis, unless fewer than 3 would be left;
    • the share of all its papers that name the gene or disease.
    It names all four ends at once, so that the two axes don’t get the same names. Without it, each end is named by its 3 most distinctive words. The names are cut to fit the plot, and are given in full in the note.
  4. Small maps. With fewer than 1,000 papers, a few papers can change the up axis a lot: the note says so.

Methods tested

Before settling the maps’ methods, we tested them on all 7.8 million papers about human genes (every PubMed paper PubTator3 tags with an HGNC gene), using the same 384-number MedEmbed-small embeddings as the search. The question: when the embeddings are squeezed down to a few dimensions, to cluster and to draw them, how much of their structure survives? The results led to the MedEmbed map tab, and support the Map tab’s settings. Full write-up and code: geometry/README.md.

The scores, each measured on 20,000 randomly chosen papers, against their true nearest neighbours (most similar papers, by cosine, over all 7.8 million):

  • Kept neighbours: of a paper’s 15 true nearest neighbours, the share still among its 15 nearest after the squeeze.
  • Share a gene: of a paper’s 15 nearest neighbours, the share tagged with at least one of its genes. This measures biology rather than geometry; the full 384-number space scores 61%, the most a squeeze can expect.
  • Same field: of neighbour pairs where both papers have a journal field, the share with the same field.
  • Far stays far: rank correlation of distances before and after the squeeze, over 100,000 random pairs of papers (1 = perfect).

Clustering

HDBSCAN on the 12-dimension UMAP, fitted on a 1 million paper sample. Together: of a paper’s 15 true nearest neighbours, the share in its cluster (unclustered counts as a miss). Gene lift: how many times more often two papers in one cluster share a gene than two random papers. Unclustered: papers HDBSCAN leaves out as noise. A selection of the 14 settings tried:

Every HDBSCAN setting left 40–60% of papers unclustered, so we fitted on a sample and then placed every paper by a vote of its neighbours. Scored on 20,000 papers outside the sample, the chosen 780 clusters still keep 80% of true neighbours together. Drawn on the t-SNE map, which never saw them, the clusters land as coherent patches.
